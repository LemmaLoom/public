Counts, shares, and rates#

A count is a measurement representing the occurrences of something. For instance, you could measure 20 occurrences of hedges in “Radio Debate”, or 6 codings of “Urgency” within the “Activists” set. All measurements in LemmaLoom stem directly or indirectly from a count.

Share, on the other hand, is the proportion between parts and the whole they are contained within. Shares run between 0 and 1 and are most commonly expressed as percentages. Share measures in LemmaLoom include coverage, Lexical density, the Share measure for queries, and the percentages used in a Code Matrix. Suppose “Costs” codings span 250 characters out of the 5,000 characters of “Running Out of Time”. Thus, the coverage share for “Costs” equals , which means 5.0%.

If you need to calculate the rate of occurrence for a whole group of documents, you can calculate either the pooled or document-specific rates. Pooled rates take the total number of occurrences divided by the total word counts. Long documents therefore carry greater weight than shorter ones. The rows of a query show these rates. The alternative method calculates individual rates and ranks them against each other. The rank test in Compare uses this method (see Tests later in this chapter).

p-values#

In general, there will almost always be some discrepancy between two groups’ usage rates of a particular word, regardless of whether there is anything to distinguish one group from another. This discrepancy can be assessed statistically in order to test its significance, and p-values range from 0 to 1 to express the probability of a difference at least this large if the groups did not really differ. If your p-value is low, then the discrepancy is hard to put down to randomness alone.

Log-likelihood for a keyword list in LemmaLoom’s queries (see How the two scores are calculated in Chapter 16, Query Results and Statistics) is twice the sum of the products of each observed frequency and the natural logarithm of its ratio to the expected frequency. Note that measures roughly the same departure from expectations that the chi-square statistic does, with differences in methodology and values being fairly minimal; compare 5.34 to the earlier 4.68.

At the observed of 5.34, there is roughly a 0.021 probability that a difference of this size or larger occurs simply by chance, when both groups use hope at the same rate. Out of every 100 instances where the difference between both groups’ usage rate was zero, you might expect about two instances like this one, so hope passes the threshold LL ≥ 3.84 but not LL ≥ 6.63.

P-values of 0.05, 0.01, and 0.001 are typically chosen for significance; any p-value below 0.05 is often considered statistically significant. The threshold p-value of 0.05 is used by LemmaLoom wherever it shows significance (see How significance is shown later in this chapter).

A p-value of 0.021 is not equivalent to a 98% probability that there is indeed a difference. A p-value does not indicate the magnitude of the effect; rather, the effect size is used for this purpose (see Effect sizes below). Neither does a p-value greater than 0.05 show equivalence between the two groups; with a limited number of documents, a sizable difference could slip past without reaching statistical significance.

A p-value also makes an assumption that there was only one test conducted. A keyword list runs one for every word. For example, if 1,000 words that show no real difference undergo a hypothesis test, about 50 (5 in 100) will appear to be statistically significant at LL ≥ 3.84 purely by coincidence. More conservative criteria like LL ≥ 6.63 only admit roughly 10 words in the same circumstances, whereas LL ≥ 10.83 admits about 1.

Most hypothesis tests require comparing the calculated value against a distribution. The distribution provides the likelihood, for every value of the test statistic, of obtaining that particular outcome when the groups do not differ; the p-value corresponds to the portion of the distribution beyond that point. Normal distributions are used for the rank test for two groups. Chi-square distributions are used for the rank test for more groups, chi-square tests, and keyword thresholds.

Unlike other tests, Fisher’s exact test calculates the actual p-value of the test statistic rather than estimating it approximately. All arrangements of cells that retain the marginal sums are accounted for, adding their probabilities together for outcomes no more likely than the observation. As such, it remains a valid approach for cases where chi-square distributions are known to fit poorly, specifically those where the expected counts are relatively low (under five or so, by a common rule of thumb).

A two-sided test considers deviations from expectations in both directions, whereas a one-sided test accounts for one direction alone. Compare in queries and the Code Matrix employ two-sided Fisher’s exact tests. Conversely, the Sketch Space feature uses one-sided tests, which look only for more relations than expected, as does the Multimode mode of the Word Sketch.

Ranks#

Some tests are ranking-based. That means each value receives a rank based upon its ordering. Four documents whose rates are 22, 30, 30, and 41 would have ranks of 1, 2.5, 2.5, and 4. Ranks of 2 and 3 would be taken up by both 30s; thus, they share the average of .

Note how ranks disregard spacing between values. If the maximum rate changes from 41 to 410, its rank remains unchanged at 4. That is why one document with an extreme rate cannot determine the outcome of the rank test on its own.

Document statistics#

The Overview facet of the inspector’s Text tab (⌥⌘1) provides access to the statistics of the active document once it has been indexed. All statistics are listed according to five sections titled Overview, Readability, Lexical, Syntax, and Structure. Bar graphs of Parts of speech, Functions, and Phrases come afterward. The statistics of Type-token ratio, MATTR, and Mean dependency distance are all displayed with two decimal places, while LIX is displayed as a whole number. Hapax ratio and the shares are whole percentages, while the remaining statistics are shown with one decimal place. Queries provide access to these statistics through the document field Statistic, and thus allow filtering or grouping based on statistical properties (see Finding documents by a statistic in Chapter 10, The Text Inspector).

Here, words means word tokens; this measure does not account for punctuation symbols or other non-word characters like #, but includes numbers. Sentences refer to sentences identified during indexing by LemmaLoom.

Syntax#

The Syntax section appears once LemmaLoom has analyzed the grammatical structure of the sentences (see Syntax in Chapter 10, The Text Inspector). Dependency analysis establishes which word, its head, each word depends upon within each individual sentence; for example, adjectives depend on nouns, subjects depend on verbs, et cetera. Everything connects to a head except the root, which is most often the main verb of the sentence.

With a document-level key such as “Stance”, the words and characters of a row are those contained within the documents with that value. With any other key, such as Code, they are the words and characters contained within all documents that were read by the current query.

With a key set to Position in Document, which is set by Over Time whenever the key is left blank, a strip will be drawn depicting the hits in each document, along with two figures above the strips pertaining to how these hits are distributed across documents (see Where hits fall in each document in Chapter 16, Query Results and Statistics). If the key refers to a date field or Media Time, Over Time draws the chosen measure as a line chart.

φ for overlapping codes#

The Co-occurrences section of the Code Stats facet lists every pair of codes having overlapping segments in the document, ranked with the highest φ value first. Each row shows φ itself and the number of overlapping pairs of segments, and any pair having a p-value less than 0.05 receives a * after its names. Co-occurrences in a document in Chapter 13, Analyzing Codes works through the calculation. When computing φ in the Relations view of the shelf, the formula works the same way and uses the characters of all documents in the scope.

The table underlying φ assumes that every single character in the document represents one observation. “Running Out of Time” consists of roughly 5,000 characters. For simplicity’s sake, this example assumes “Costs” takes up 250 characters, “Responsibility” 200, while both share 100 characters:

The expected overlap is characters. The codes have ten times as much overlap as that. The other three cells expect 240, 190, and 4,560, so every cell lies 90 from its expected count:

To calculate φ, divide χ² by and take the square root. Here, represents how many characters were in the document.

The p-value was derived from the chi-square distribution with one degree of freedom, giving you a p far below 0.001.

With thousands of characters being your observations, the p-value will tend to be tiny for almost any overlap, and neighboring characters are hardly independent. Within a long document, nearly every pair has a *. You thus need to evaluate pairs based on their φ, not their *. This becomes true to an even greater extent in Relations, since the characters of all the documents in the scope get pooled together.

φ has no sign. Should one pair overlap less often than chance predicts, you’d still end up getting a φ above 0, and often a *. If “Costs” and “Responsibility” had shared only 2 characters, just one-fifth of the 10 expected, their φ would come out at roughly 0.04, with a *. To distinguish between attraction and avoidance, check the observed overlap versus the expected overlap. The CSV export provides you with the former under the Characters column. Expected overlap is simply the characters of one code multiplied by the characters of the other code, divided by the characters of the document.

Choosing a test in Chapter 16, Query Results and Statistics sets out which test should be used for each individual question.

Compare, the Code Matrix, and Sketch Space are three different tools within LemmaLoom that conduct tests. The following describes what is tested by each tool:

  • Compare tests documents. When using one Group By key, Compare tests only when the key is document-level, such as attributes like “Stance” or the document’s Group itself. Tests available in the menu showing Automatic include Automatic, Rank Test on Rates, Presence Test, Chi-Square on Counts, and No Test. If the measure equals Documents, Automatic chooses the presence test; otherwise, the rank test is chosen instead.
  • The rank test measures each document’s rate per 10,000 words or per 10,000 characters if the query did not count words, whatever the rows show. This is shown in more detail in Running a comparison in Chapter 16, Query Results and Statistics. All the documents get counted regardless of hits; documents without hits have a rate of 0. The presence test counts how many documents have hits vs no hits, which means you must have at least one of each type for the test to run.
  • Should you specify two Group By keys, Compare will analyze the table formed by Group By rather than individual documents. In that scenario, Automatic will perform chi-square testing only when Find is Documents or any other unit that belongs to the Library group, while Chi-Square on Counts and Presence Test will run the test against table cells. Rank Test on Rates works with one key only.
  • The Code Matrix applies the hypothesis tests to each code separately, whatever the two switches show; specifically, the null hypothesis says that there is no difference between the shares of documents containing each particular code within each group. Groups with No value (or Ungrouped for document groups) are skipped over as part of testing, whereas Compare treats them as a group of their own. If a code is in all or none of the analyzed documents, its test cells display a dash.
  • Sketch Space marks a cell with * only if it contains at least 3 relations, more than what would be expected. Moreover, this difference in frequency must be statistically significant enough that the p-value of the one-sided test is less than 0.05.

How significance is shown#

Wherever LemmaLoom highlights significance, it uses the cutoff p < 0.05, with these differences in formatting for display:

  • Compare prints the test line darker when p is below 0.05, and gray otherwise. It displays the statistic rounded to two decimal places, while displaying the p-value and effect size rounded to three decimal places. Any value of p smaller than 0.001 is displayed as p < 0.001.
  • The Code Matrix darkens the test values associated with a particular code if they are significant (p < 0.05); additionally, a * is appended after the p-value. The statistic and Cramér’s V will both display their values to two decimal places, whereas p-values will display to three decimal places, except values smaller than 0.001, which display as < 0.001.
  • Relations marks any cells which are significant within Grid (displayed via a * in the corner of the cell) and Pairs (displayed via a * after the p-value); p-values smaller than 0.05 are also printed darker in Pairs.
  • The Code Stats facet adds a * after the names of a pair of codes.
  • Sketch Space and the Multimode mode of the Word Sketch add a * next to the count within the grid cells. Beneath the grid is a line indicating what this mark means.

In keyword lists, any words not reaching the log-likelihood chosen in the menu showing LL ≥ 3.84 are hidden.

The numbers presented in the table assume no tied rates between any documents; ties can lower p and raise r considerably. With four documents without hits against two documents with different rates, for example, p is 0.028 and r is 0.894.

The maximum r value also depends on the sizes of the two groups. Without tied rates, the value of r never reaches 0.87, and it stays far lower when the two groups differ greatly in size.
