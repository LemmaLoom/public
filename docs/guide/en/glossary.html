Glossary

These are the definitions shown in Help ▸ Glossary, grouped as in the app.

Concepts#

Analyzer: A module that runs during indexing and adds findings, such as cohesion or rhetoric, to a document.

Anatomy: A bar showing how a sentence’s words divide among its clause slots, such as subject, verb, and objects.

Attribute: Attributes describe your documents; for example author, wave, or rating.

Bookmark: A marked passage to return to, with an optional comment.

Code: A label for a theme or idea that you apply to passages of text.

Code format: How coded text is drawn in the reader: highlighted, underlined, or boxed.

Code Matrix: A table of how often each code is applied in each document.

Code scope: Whether a code belongs to the whole project, to one document set, or to one document.

Code set: A named collection of codes for filtering and comparing.

Code Stats: Frequencies and coverage of codes across a scope.

Code System: The hierarchy of all codes in a project.

Codebook: The codes of a project with their descriptions and how they are used across a scope.

Coded memo: A memo attached to a passage and assigned to a code, whose attributes and tags it inherits.

Codeline: Codes present in each sentence, top to bottom.

Coding: One application of a code to a passage of text.

Constituency: Phrase structure: how words group into nested phrases.

Custom analyzer: An analyzer written in JavaScript.

Dependency: A grammatical relation between a word and the word it depends on, its head.

Dictionary: Categories of terms counted across the documents of a project.

Document: A text, web page, PDF, or transcribed recording in a project.

Document set: A named collection of documents that can serve as the scope of an analysis; a document can be in many sets.

Exclude from indexing: Matching text is skipped by indexing, but stays visible in the reader and can still be coded.

Form × Function: A matrix of which forms, such as noun phrases or clauses, do which grammatical jobs.

Group: A folder in the navigator for organizing documents; a document belongs to at most one group.

Indexing: The grammar analysis of a document: sentences, tokens, parts of speech, dependencies, and phrase structure.

Lemma: The dictionary form of a word, such as run for ran and running.

Lens: A layer that colors or annotates the text in the reader by a grammatical or analytic category.

Link: A connection between two passages, with an optional relation and comment.

Memo: A note attached to a passage, a document, a code, or the project.

Mosaic: Code colors in reading order, sized by segment length.

Portrait: A visual overview of where codes appear in a document.

Project: A collection of documents with its own codes, sets, attributes, and dictionaries.

REFI-QDA Codebook .qdc: A standard file format for exchanging code systems between qualitative analysis programs.

REFI-QDA Project .qdpx: A standard file format for exchanging whole projects between qualitative analysis programs.

Relations: A table of how often codes overlap in the same passages.

Segment: The passage of text that a coding covers.

Subcode: A code nested under another code.

Tag: A free-form label for organizing projects, documents, codes, memos, and links.

Token: A single word or punctuation mark as the parser segments the text.

Transcript: The text of a recording, with each passage linked to its time in the media.

Reader and lenses#

Bookmarks: Shows bookmarked passages.

Codes: Shows coded segments in their code colors and formats.

Cohesion: Shows how each sentence ties back to what came before.

Connectives: Annotates words that link ideas, grouped by the relation they express.

Constructions: Annotates characteristic Chinese constructions such as 把 and 被. (Chinese)

Grammatical function: Colors words by the job they do in the sentence, such as subject or object.

Metadiscourse: Annotates words with which writers guide readers, such as hedges and boosters.

Modality: Annotates expressions of ability, possibility, necessity, obligation, and volition.

Morphology: Annotates grammatical features such as tense, number, and case. (English, German)

Negation: Annotates negators and the part of the sentence they negate.

Particles: Annotates Chinese aspect particles, structural particles, and classifiers. (Chinese)

Phrase: Annotates phrases such as noun phrases and prepositional phrases.

Rhetoric: Annotates rhetorical devices found by the Rhetoric analyzer.

Sentence fields: Annotates the topological fields of German sentences. (German)

Word class: Colors each word by its part of speech.

Grammar#

Ability: Modal meaning of being able to do something.

Additive: A connective that adds information to what came before.

Adjectival Adj'l: A word or phrase that modifies a noun, doing an adjective’s job.

Adverbial Adv'l: A word, phrase, or clause that modifies a verb, adjective, or clause, telling how, when, where, or why.

Adversative: A connective that signals contrast or opposition.

Agent: In a passive clause, the doer of the action, usually introduced by by.

Appositive Appos: A noun phrase next to another that renames it, as in my friend, a teacher.

Be + adverbial S · be · ADV: “Be” linking the subject to a place or time. (English)

Be + complement S · be · SC: “Be” linking the subject to a noun or adjective that renames or describes it. (English)

Causal: A connective that signals cause, reason, or result.

Classifier: A measure word required between a number or demonstrative and a noun. (Chinese)

Complement Comp: A phrase or clause that a word needs to complete its meaning, such as the clause after decide.

Complex transitive S · V · DO · OC: A direct object plus a complement that describes it. (English)

Concessive: A connective that concedes a point without giving up the main claim.

Conditional: A connective that introduces a condition.

Conjunct Conj: An element joined by coordination to another of the same kind, such as dogs in cats and dogs.

Connector: A word that links clauses or phrases, such as a conjunction or a relative pronoun.

Ditransitive S · V · IO · DO: A verb with both an indirect and a direct object (give, send, tell...). (English)

Existential 有: 有 used to state that something exists or is present somewhere. (Chinese)

Fragment —: Not a complete independent clause. (English)

Imperative (You) · V: A command, with the subject “you” understood. (English)

Intransitive S · V: A verb with no object; the action stays with the subject. (English)

Linke Klammer: The left bracket of the German sentence frame: the finite verb in main clauses, the subordinating conjunction in subordinate clauses. (German)

Linking S · LV · SC: A linking verb (seem, become, taste...) joining the subject to a complement that describes it. (English)

Main clause: The independent clause a sentence is built around; its verb is the root of the dependency tree.

Mittelfeld: The field between the two brackets, holding objects, adverbials, and often the subject. (German)

Nachfeld: The position after the right bracket, often holding extraposed clauses. (German)

Necessity: Modal meaning of something being necessary or certain.

Negation scope: The stretch of the sentence that a negation applies to.

Negator: A word that negates, such as not, never, or no.

Object Obj: A noun phrase that completes a verb or preposition, typically the one affected by the action.

Obligation: Modal meaning of a duty or requirement placed on someone.

Possibility: Modal meaning of something being possible or likely.

Predicative Pred: A phrase after a linking verb that describes or identifies the subject, as in she is a doctor.

Question ?: A direct question. (English)

Rechte Klammer: The right bracket: non-finite verbs, separated verb particles, or the finite verb in subordinate clauses. (German)

Subject Subj: The constituent a clause is about; usually it agrees with the verb and performs the action.

Subjunctive: The German verb mood (Konjunktiv) for reported speech, hypotheses, and polite requests. (German)

Temporal: A connective that orders events in time.

Transitive S · V · DO: A verb acting on a direct object. (English)

Volition: Modal meaning of wanting or intending.

Vorfeld: The position before the finite verb in a German main clause; it holds one constituent, often the topic. (German)

了 perfective: Aspect particle marking a completed action. (Chinese)

地 adverbial: Particle linking an adverbial modifier to its verb. (Chinese)

得 complement: Particle introducing a complement of degree or result after a verb. (Chinese)

把 disposal: The 把 construction moves the object before the verb to show what is done to it. (Chinese)

是…的 focus: The 是…的 frame highlights the time, place, or manner of a past event. (Chinese)

的 attributive: Particle linking a modifier to the noun it describes. (Chinese)

着 durative: Aspect particle marking an ongoing action or a resulting state. (Chinese)

被 passive: The 被 construction marks a passive, with the affected participant as subject. (Chinese)

过 experiential: Aspect particle marking that something has been experienced at least once. (Chinese)

Text statistics#

Automated Readability Index ARI: Grade estimate from characters per word and words per sentence. (English)

Avg. sentence length: The average number of words per sentence.

Avg. tree depth: Average depth of the deepest dependency chain per sentence.

Avg. word length: The average number of letters per word, or characters per word in Chinese.

Characters: The number of characters in the text.

Characters per sentence: The average number of characters per sentence; a length measure suited to Chinese. (Chinese)

Clauses per sentence: Average number of clause constituents per sentence.

Co-occurrence: Two codes applied to overlapping passages of the same document.

Coleman-Liau: Grade estimate from letters and sentences per 100 words; no syllable counting. (English)

Coverage: The share of a text’s characters covered by a code, counting overlapping segments once.

Dialogue share: The share of words inside quotation marks; estimated from quote pairing.

Embedded clause share: The share of clauses nested inside another clause.

Flesch Reading Ease: 0–100 scale; higher is easier. 60–70 reads as plain English. Syllables are estimated. (English)

Flesch-Amstad: The German adaptation of Flesch Reading Ease; higher is easier. Syllables are estimated. (German)

Flesch-Kincaid Grade: US school grade needed to read the text comfortably. Syllables are estimated. (English)

Gunning Fog: Years of schooling needed; counts long sentences and words of three or more syllables. Syllables are estimated. (English)

Hapax ratio: The share of unique words that occur exactly once.

Läsbarhetsindex LIX: Sentence length plus the share of words over six letters; 40 is average difficulty. (English, German)

Lexical density: The share of content words (not the, of, is); higher reads as denser.

Mean dependency distance MDD: Average distance between a word and its syntactic head; larger distances are harder to process.

Moving-average type-token ratio MATTR: Type-token ratio averaged over a moving 100-word window, so it compares across texts of different lengths.

Paragraphs: The number of paragraphs, divided at blank lines when the text has any, and otherwise at every line break.

Particle density: Particles such as 的, 了, and 着 per 100 words. (Chinese)

Passive sentences: The share of sentences containing a passive construction.

Questions and exclamations: The share of sentences that end in a question mark or exclamation mark.

Sentences: The number of sentences the parser found.

Sentences per paragraph: The average number of sentences in each paragraph.

Simple Measure of Gobbledygook SMOG: Grade estimate from the number of words with three or more syllables per sentence, normalized to 30 sentences. Syllables are estimated. (English)

Stopword share: The share of function words like the, of, and is.

Type-token ratio TTR: Unique words divided by total words; higher means more varied vocabulary.

Unique words: The number of distinct lemmas (types).

Wiener Sachtextformel: German school-grade estimate from syllables, sentence length, and word length. Syllables are estimated. (German)

Words: The number of word tokens, not counting punctuation and spaces.

Association measures#

Chi-square test χ²: Tests whether the share of documents with a code differs between three or more groups more than chance would explain; documents without a value are left out.

Cramér’s V V: The strength of the difference the chi-square test measures, from 0 (no difference) to 1.

Cubed mutual information MI3: A variant of mutual information that cubes the observed count, reducing its bias toward rare pairs.

Dice coefficient Dice: Twice the co-occurrence count divided by the sum of the partner’s frequency and the node’s total (the words in its windows for collocates, or its pairings in that relation for word sketches); ranges from 0 to 1.

Expected: The number of co-occurrences to expect if the two words were independent.

Fisher exact test: A significance test for small counts, used to find characteristic relations in Sketch Space and to compare two groups in the Code Matrix.

Frequency f: How often two words occur together: within the window in Collocates, or linked in the relation in a word sketch.

Log-likelihood LL: A significance statistic (G²) comparing observed with expected co-occurrence; higher means stronger evidence of association.

logDice: 14 plus the base-2 logarithm of the Dice coefficient; its maximum is 14 and it compares well across corpora of different sizes.

Mutual information MI: The base-2 logarithm of observed over expected co-occurrence: how much more often two words meet than by chance.

p-value p: The probability of an association at least this strong arising by chance; smaller values are stronger evidence.

Phi coefficient φ: The strength of association between two codes, from 0 to 1, computed from how their passages overlap.

t-score: Observed minus expected co-occurrence, divided by the square root of the observed count; favors frequent, dependable pairs.

z-score: Observed minus expected co-occurrence, divided by the square root of the expected count.

Word sketches#

Adjective modifiers: Adjectives that modify the sketched noun.

Adverb modifiers: Adverbs that modify the sketched word.

Characteristic relation *: A relation that a code or group of documents uses significantly more often than the rest of the scope (one-sided Fisher exact test, p < .05).

Code Sketch: A word sketch whose partners are grouped by the codes that cover them.

Collocate: A word that occurs near another word more often than chance would predict.

Complement of: Words that the sketched word completes.

Complements: Words or clauses that complete the sketched word.

Coordinated with: Words joined to the sketched word by and, or, and similar conjunctions.

In compounds with: Words that form compounds with the sketched word.

Modifiers: In Sketch Space, all modifying relations combined: Adjective modifiers, Adverb modifiers, and Modifies.

Modifies: Words that the sketched word modifies.

Object of: Verbs that take the sketched noun as their object.

Objects: Nouns that act as the object of the sketched verb.

Prepositional phrases: Prepositional phrases attached to the sketched word, grouped by preposition.

Sketch Space: A comparison of a word’s grammatical partners across codes or groups of documents, marking the relations that are characteristic of each.

Subject of: Verbs that take the sketched noun as their subject.

Subjects: Nouns that act as the subject of the sketched verb.

Word Sketch: A one-page summary of a word’s grammatical partners, grouped by relation and ranked by logDice.

Discourse#

Absolute phrase: A noun + participle standing apart from the main clause, zooming in on one detail. (English)

Anaphora: Consecutive sentences opening with the same words, building rhythm and insistence through repetition.

Appositive: A noun phrase set beside another to rename it, adding information without spending a whole clause.

Attitude marker: Signals the writer’s stance toward the content (unfortunately, remarkably).

Booster: Closes down doubt; commits the writer fully (clearly, certainly).

Cohesion check: The known to new contract; each sentence should open on familiar ground.

Connective: Steers the reader between ideas (however, therefore, meanwhile).

Correlative pair: Paired conjunctions that throw extra weight onto the joined elements.

Demonstrative tie: The theme points back with a demonstrative, such as this or these.

Engagement: Pulls the reader in directly (you, we, consider, imagine).

Final particle: Sentence-final stance marker (吗, 呢, 吧, 啊). (Chinese)

Focus construction: there-transformations, what-clefts, and it-clefts that reshape the sentence to drop new information into the stress position.

Fronted opener: Opens with an adverbial or participial phrase instead of the subject, setting the scene before the actor arrives.

Hedge: Softens a claim; opens space for doubt (perhaps, may, suggests).

Modal particle: Tones the utterance toward the listener (ja, doch, eben, halt). (German)

New topic: The theme does not tie back to the two sentences before, or the sentence has no theme at all; a possible break in cohesion.

Nominalization: An action packaged as a noun (decide -> decision). Hides who is doing what and stacks abstractions; unpack to recover a live verb.

Opening: The first sentence of the document, which has nothing earlier to tie back to.

Parallelism: Coordinated elements built to the same grammatical shape (a tricolon, a balanced pair) so the form carries the meaning.

Passive voice: The doer slips out of subject position, foregrounding the receiver.

Periodic sentence: The main verb arrives late, after stacked modifiers, holding the reader in suspension until the clause resolves. (English, German)

Possessive tie: The theme refers back with a possessive, such as its or their.

Power word: A superlative or intensified word that seizes the stress and turns a fact into an arguable claim.

Pronoun tie: The sentence’s theme refers back with a pronoun to something already mentioned.

Question / command: A shift out of plain statement; a question that pulls the reader in, an imperative that addresses them directly.

Repeated word: The theme repeats a content word from the previous sentences.

Zero anaphora: The subject is left out and understood from the previous sentence; common in Chinese. (Chinese)
